**Standfirst.** Ask what Nikola Tesla's high-frequency machines actually were and you
usually get a coil and a guessed number. The 1894 volume has something better: a detailed
description of rotary alternators with hundreds of iron poles, the speeds they were run at,
the currents they gave, and a list of the ways they failed. Chapter XXIX runs from p. 374
to p. 391 and it is the most engineering-dense material in the book.

![1894 engraving, Fig. 199: Tesla's high-frequency alternator in side elevation, a large annular magnetic frame carried on a four-armed spider with a bolted base, the zigzag field winding visible in the rim grooves](/assets/tesla1894/p375-fig199.webp "Fig. 199, printed p. 375: the machine in side elevation. The key on the same page states 'A is an annular magnetic frame, the interior of which is provided with a large number of pole-pieces D' (1894, p. 375). Public domain.")

## The problem was a noisy lamp

The chapter opens with an objection to alternating current, not with a boast. Arc lamps run
on alternating or pulsating currents "emit a pronounced sound, varying with the rate of the
alternations or pulsations of current", and the book explains why: the noise comes from "the
rapidly alternating heating and cooling, and consequent expansion and contraction, of the
gaseous matter forming the arc" (1894, p. 374). Worse, the arc's resistance rises
periodically, which makes small arcs impossible: "small arcs are impracticable"
(1894, p. 374).

Tesla's answer was to go up in frequency until the problem stopped being audible. Push the
rate of change past the limit of hearing and "the sound produced by or in the arc becomes
practically inaudible" (1894, p. 376). Fast alternation also keeps the arc hot, and a hot
arc has low resistance, so it stays lit. High-frequency arcs, the chapter says, "possess the
advantages of silence and persistency" (1894, p. 374).

That is the commercial reason for the whole chapter. Not mystery, a hum.

## The machine: an annulus with hundreds of poles

The design is in Figs. 199 and 200. "A is an annular magnetic frame, the interior of which
is provided with a large number of pole-pieces D" (1894, p. 375). Because the poles are
small and close together, you cannot wind a coil around each one. Instead the field is
wound with a single insulated conductor run "zigzag through the grooves", around and around
the ring until there are as many layers as you want, which leaves the poles energised with
alternately opposite polarity all the way round (1894, p. 375).

The armature is a spider carrying a ring, turned down into a trough and packed with "a mass
of fine annealed iron wires", with pins set in the sides of the ring and the armature coils
wound over the periphery around those pins. The coils are connected in series and their
terminals are carried out through a hollow shaft to contact rings, where brushes take the
current off (1894, p. 375).

The point of all that fiddly construction is one sentence: "In this way a machine with a
very large number of poles may be constructed" (1894, p. 375). Frequency out of a rotating
machine is poles times revolutions. If you cannot spin it faster, you add poles.

## The numbers, as the book gives them

This is the part most retellings leave out, and it is the reason the machine is worth
writing about.

- Three hundred and seventy-five to four hundred poles, driven "at a speed of fifteen
  hundred or sixteen hundred revolutions per minute, which will produce ten thousand or
  eleven thousand alternations of current per second" (1894, pp. 375, 376).
- A specific experimental machine with a wrought-iron field ring 32 inches outside
  diameter, 30 inches inside, carrying "384 polar projections" and 384 coils in two
  series. It was run "as high as 3,000 revolutions per minute" and gave "as high as 10
  amperes" (1894, p. 377).
- The limit of the simpler field design: "Up to about 15,000 alternations per second in an
  experimental machine, the former plan may be followed, but a more efficient machine is
  obtained on the second plan" (1894, p. 376).
- The second plan, shown in Fig. 206: "the field had 480 polar projections on each side,
  and from this machine it was possible to obtain 30,000 alternations per second"
  (1894, p. 381).

Thirty thousand alternations a second, out of a shaft with a belt pulley on it, described
in a book published in 1894. That is a real figure from a real book, and it beats anything
the myth needs.

## What the frequency bought him

The chapter's payoff is an experiment you could almost do in a garage, if you had a
20,000-alternation generator.

"A machine was used giving about 20,000 alternations per second. Two bare wires about twenty
feet long and two millimetres in diameter, in close proximity to each other, were connected
to the terminals of the machine at the one end, and to a condenser at the other"
(1894, p. 391). With a small air-core transformer and a Cardew voltmeter, the electromotive
force read about 120 volts at the condenser and about 65 volts at the machine, falling
"inch by inch" along the wires. The writer's conclusion is honest rather than triumphant:
"The writer looked for a case of resonance, but he was unable to augment the effect by
varying the capacity very carefully and gradually or by changing the speed of the machine. A
case of pure resonance he was unable to obtain" (1894, p. 391).

In a lecture printed earlier in the same volume, Tesla puts the same capability the other
way: at "a few thousand volts there is no particular difficulty encountered in conveying
currents from a machine giving, say, 20,000 alternations per second, to quite a distance",
and then adds the caveat that matters, "This number of alternations, however, is by far too
small for many purposes, though quite sufficient for some practical applications"
(1894, p. 151).

What he then did with a line like that on one conductor only, and why the return path in those
demonstrations was the room rather than a second wire, is worked through on its own page at
[Tesla's One-Wire Power: The Experiment Textbooks Skip](tesla-one-wire-power.html), which quotes the
same volume's arithmetic for why one wire was the economical choice at high frequency
(1894, p. 267).

## "A tale of woe": why the machine was hard

Martin reprints Tesla writing in the *Electrical World* in February 1891 about building
these generators, and it is funny because it is exact. Anyone attempting such a machine for
the first time "will have a tale of woe to tell". He starts, naturally, by making an
armature with the required number of poles, and gets "an apparatus which is fit to
accompany a thoroughly Wagnerian opera", one that may "possess the virtue of converting
mechanical energy into heat in a nearly perfect manner" (1894, p. 381).

The heat is the story. Iron at high frequency is a heater unless you build it out of
lamination and thin wire: "As the polar projections must necessarily be very narrow, very
thin wires or sheets must be used to avoid the eddy current effects" (1894, p. 381). In the
384-pole machine "it was found impracticable to use any wire much thicker than No. 26 B.
and S. gauge on account of the local effects" (1894, p. 377). The armature wires had to be
wound with care "as they are apt to fly off in consequence of the great peripheral speed"
(1894, p. 377). Insulation was its own problem: with a gas present, discharge can work
"through as much as an inch of the best solid insulating material" (1894, p. 151).

The chapter ends by naming what Tesla hoped would replace these machines: the oscillator,
which its inventor believed would give "the necessary high frequency conditions, while
relieving him of the inconveniences that attach to generators of the type described at the
beginning of this chapter" (1894, p. 391). That machine has its own record, and its own
afterlife as a legend: [the earthquake machine, from the 1893 documents](tesla-earthquake-machine.html). The exhibit where both were shown is
[here](tesla-worlds-fair-1893.html).

## What the internet gets wrong

**A single "Tesla coil frequency".** There isn't one. The rotary machines in this chapter
run from a few thousand to 30,000 alternations per second depending on poles and speed, and
Tesla calls 20,000 too small for many purposes (1894, p. 151). A page that quotes one
number for "a Tesla coil" is describing a circuit, not a machine, and usually guessing.

**That high frequency was always about wireless power.** In this chapter it is about arc
lamps: silence, and the ability to run a small arc (1894, p. 374). The wireless material is
elsewhere in the book, and it comes later.

**That the machine was a curiosity.** It ran "Mr. Tesla's laboratory lights" and was
"capable of running two arcs of normal candle power" (1894, pp. 377, 376). It was a working
laboratory supply.

**That the captions in this book can be trusted.** Our own extraction tool read the caption
under Fig. 199 as "Fig. 203", and the one under Fig. 206 as "Fig. 207". Both printed pages
say otherwise, and we re-cropped from the scans to get the right plates. Every figure on
this page was checked against the page it came from.

## If you came here for a generator to build

The honest read of Chapter XXIX is that frequency was expensive: more poles, thinner wire,
tighter clearances, careful winding, and a machine that hums and heats if you get it wrong.
Nothing in it supports a claim that a home-built magnet machine can skip that work. These
are alternators; the motors that made the same polyphase system useful, and the measured
efficiency record that goes with them, are in
[the paper Tesla scribbled in pencil](rotating-magnetic-field-1888.html). If you want the
practical version of this subject, read [what a magnetic generator actually
costs](magnetic-generator-plans-review.html) and [how a dynamo generator is put
together](magnetic-dynamo-generator.html).
