**Standfirst.** Nikola Tesla lit lamps by holding them, in New York in 1891 and in
London in 1892, and the 1894 book prints both lectures. It also prints the tests he
ran to decide what was actually happening. Those tests are better than the legend.

## The demonstration he gave twice

In February 1892 Nikola Tesla addressed the Institution of Electrical Engineers in
London, and partway into the lecture he picked up a glass tube.

"Here is a simple glass tube from which the air has been partially exhausted. I take
hold of it ; I bring my body in contact with a wire conveying alternating currents of
high potential, and the tube in my hand is brilliantly lighted." (1894, p. 200)

Then the sentence that turns a curiosity into a demonstration: "In whatever position I
may put it, wherever I move it in space, as far as I can reach, its soft, pleasing
light persists with undiminished brightness." (1894, p. 200)

He followed it with a second piece of apparatus. "Here is an exhausted bulb suspended
from a single wire. Standing on an insulated support, I grasp it, and a platinum button
mounted in it is brought to vivid incandescence." (1894, p. 200)

None of this was news to that room. Nine months earlier he had given the same class of
results to the American Institute of Electrical Engineers, at Columbia College in New
York, on May 20, 1891 (1894, p. 145). That lecture is chapter XXVI of the book, the
London one is chapter XXVII, and he refers back to the earlier paper in the first pages
of the later: "A short time ago I had the honor to bring before our American Institute
of Electrical Engineers some results then arrived at by me in a novel line of work."
(1894, p. 199)

The 1891 text states the same effect without the stagecraft: "if a tube be taken in one
hand, the observer being near the coil, it is brilliantly lighted and remains so no
matter in what position it is held relatively to the observer's body" (1894, p. 185).

The claim is in print twice, with the apparatus described and the audience named. The
interesting part is what he did next.

## The return path is the room

A lamp needs a circuit. Every reader knows that, and it is why a one-terminal lamp
sounds like conjuring. Tesla's answer is not that the rule is wrong. It is that the
second side of the circuit can be the space around the apparatus.

"When a lamp is lighted by connecting it to one terminal only of the source, this may be
facilitated by providing the globe with an outside condenser coating, which serves at
the same time as a reflector, and connecting this to an insulated body of some size."
(1894, p. 183)

Then the line that gives the game away: "In all cases the insulated body serves to give
off the energy into the surrounding space, and is equivalent to a return wire."
(1894, p. 184) He adds that "instead of connecting the wires to an insulated body,
connections may be made to the ground" (1894, p. 184).

The numbers are in the same chapter. To drive energy through the wall of a glass lamp by
electrostatic induction, "using currents alternating twenty thousand times a second, the
potential required is approximately 9,000 volts" (1894, p. 183). Nine thousand volts,
twenty thousand alternations a second, and no second wire. That is not mysticism. It is
a transformer working outside the range where low-frequency wiring feels intuitive.

Fig. 124 is the wiring for exactly that lamp, and the book says the brightness is set by
the size of the plate he hangs it in: "The brilliancy of the lamp may, in this case, be
regulated within wide limits by varying the size of the insulated metal plate to which
the coating is connected." (1894, p. 183) A dimmer made out of a sheet of metal.

## He could wipe the light out with his hand

The tests in chapter XXVI are worth reading because they are built to fail in one case
and not the other.

If the tube were driven by electromagnetic induction, the body of the person holding it
would get in the way. It does not: "Were the action electromagnetic, the tube could not
be lighted when the observer's body is interposed between it and the coil"
(1894, p. 185). At a distance a person can complete the effect instead of spoiling it.
"the observer may, standing upon an insulated support between coil and tube, light the
latter by approaching the hand to it ; or he may even render it luminous by simply
stepping between it and the coil. This would be impossible with electro-magnetic
induction, for the body of the observer would act as a screen." (1894, p. 186)

Then the effect an audience remembered: "the singular effect of wiping out the light of
the tube may be produced by passing the hand quickly along the tube and at the same time
withdrawing it gently from the coil, judging properly the distance so that the tube
remains dark afterwards" (1894, p. 186).

He did not stop at the trick. He put plates in the way and watched. An insulated metal
plate interposed along the axis of the coil usually makes the tube brighter. A plate
connected to earth destroys it: "its interposition will always make the tube go out even
if it be very near the coil" (1894, p. 187). A sheet of hard rubber also quenches it,
because it "diminishes considerably the electrification through the air" (1894, p. 187).

And there is a null point. Held exactly over the centre of a coil wound in sections, the
tube "may remain completely dark", and the reason is stated: "both halves of the coil
neutralize each other, and the electric potential is zero" (1894, p. 185). Move it
slightly to one side and it lights intensely. A phenomenon with a zero crossing is a
measurable thing, not a marvel.

By the end of the section he is reading by this light: "tubes devoid of any electrodes
may be used, and there is no difficulty in producing by their means sufficient light to
read by" (1894, p. 187). In the 1892 lecture the same field drives small machines. A
tube sealed around a filament and a tiny mica fan: "When the glass tube is held anywhere
in the electrostatic field the platinum wire becomes incandescent, and the mica vanes
are rotated very fast." (1894, p. 289)

## Why he opened and closed with it

"Of all these phenomena observed with currents, or electric impulses, of high frequency,
the most fascinating for an audience are certainly those which are noted in an
electrostatic field acting through considerable distance; and the best an unskilled
lecturer can do is to begin and finish with the exhibition of these singular effects."
(1894, p. 288)

That is candour about lecture technique, and then, in the same paragraph, he reports
something he could not explain. A tube with too high a vacuum will not light in the
electrostatic field until it has been excited by a disruptive discharge coil, and it
keeps the habit: "I may put it away for a few weeks or months, still it retains the
faculty of being excited. What change have I produced in the tube in the act of exciting
it?" (1894, p. 288) He weighs two explanations, motion imparted to atoms and strain in
the dielectric, and says neither accounts for it.

One caution for anyone retelling these evenings. The book names the society, the venue
and the date, and it does not give an audience size. Any headcount you find online was
invented later.

## What the internet gets wrong

**That the hand is the power source.** It is not. The hand is one electrode, and the
capacitance of a human body is why a hand makes a difference at all. The energy comes
out of a machine, and Tesla's own phrasing is the cleanest statement of it: the
insulated body "is equivalent to a return wire" (1894, p. 184).

**That this proves energy from the aether, or free power.** Tesla did write about the
aether, and the book prints it: "The eternal recipient and transmitter of this infinite
energy is the ether." (1894, p. 145) That is his account of what a field is. It is not
the mechanism of the demonstration, and the demonstration does not depend on it. What
the lamp chapters discuss is volts, frequencies, coatings and brightness. The phrase
"free energy" does not appear anywhere in this volume; a word search of our copy of the
text returns zero hits.

**That the high voltage made the demonstration dangerous.** The book says the opposite
about the apparatus in use: "The physiological effects of the high tension discharge are
found to be so small that the shock of the coil can be supported without any
inconvenience, except perhaps a small burn produced by the discharge upon approaching the
hand to one of the terminals." (1894, p. 125) That is one man's report about one
high-frequency machine in 1891. It is not a safety instruction for anybody else, and
none of this should be tried at home.

## Where the effect survives

The lamps in these chapters lost. Electric lighting went another way, and the book is
honest that they were laboratory lamps. Of the circuit in Fig. 126: "This plan has been
used by me frequently for operating a few incandescent lamps required in the
laboratory." (1894, p. 193)

What survived is the trick of using the surroundings as part of the circuit. High-voltage
tube supplies still light a tube that is only partly connected, and touch sensors work
because a person adds capacitance to a node. The mechanism is the one drawn in Fig. 124:
a high alternating potential on one terminal, and something large enough on the other to
give that energy off into space.

The book carries the practical half of it too: generate the high tension, then step it
down before anyone touches it.
