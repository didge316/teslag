Electrical Experimenter, February 1919: Tesla's Own Words

A primary source on this site · The Electrical Experimenter, February 1919. Every page cite below is the number the magazine prints in its own corner.

Published: October 29, 2026  |  Updated: October 29, 2026

Standfirst. This is the February 1919 issue of The Electrical Experimenter, the magazine in which Nikola Tesla began writing his own life story. The PDF is on this site, and this page is the description of it that a download button cannot give: what is actually in the issue, on which printed page, how to cite it, and what a search of its text layer does and does not find.

The 1894 series on this site, The 1894 File, is built on a different document with a different page rule. This page is not part of that series and does not cite that book. It is here because the same magazine is the second place a reader of that series will want to go, and because the file has been offered to new readers by email for months without a page that says what is in it.

What Is Actually In This Issue

The issue's own masthead, printed on its contents page, reads "Vol. VI", "Whole No. 70", "FEBRUARY, 1919" and "No. 10" (EE, Feb 1919, p. 682). It was published by Experimenter Publishing Company, Inc. at 233 Fulton Street, New York, with H. Gernsback as president. The copy on this site is 84 page images.

The front cover is a colour portrait of Tesla seated beside a glowing disc. It carries the masthead, the date, the price, the line "OVER 175 ILLUST.", the words "THE TESLA WIRELESS LIGHT" and "SEE PAGE 692", and across the top, printed on the cover itself, the line "Beginning: 'My Inventions,' by Nikola Tesla". The portrait is credited on the contents page as "From a painting by George Wall" (EE, Feb 1919, p. 682).

The contents page names two articles by Tesla and one written about him:

  1. "FAMOUS SCIENTIFIC ILLUSIONS", "By Dr. Nikola Tesla", at p. 692. On the first page of the article itself the byline is "By NIKOLA TESLA" and the line under it reads "Written specially for the Electrical Experimenter" (EE, Feb 1919, p. 692).
  2. "MY INVENTIONS", at p. 696, listed on the contents page as "No. 1 of a series" and marked "Exclusive Feature." (EE, Feb 1919, p. 682).
  3. "NIKOLA TESLA THE MAN", "By H. Gernsback", on p. 697. This one is not by Tesla. It is the editor's account of meeting him, and it is where the only occurrence of 'perpetual' in the whole issue sits.

The rest of the issue is a 1919 electrical trade magazine: tidal power, rain-making by electricity, New York's crosstown subway, vacuum valve action, amateur radio, a violet-ray advertisement, and the patent list.

One correction, stated plainly. The welcome email that offers this PDF describes it as "a rare 1919 magazine packed with Nikola Tesla's articles". In this issue there are two articles by him, one cover, and one article about him by the editor. That is what is in it, and it is worth more than the inflated claim: two genuine Tesla articles, one of them his own life story, in the magazine that asked for them.

Where Tesla's Autobiography Begins

Page 696 opens with a ruled ornamental box, and inside it: "My Inventions", "By Nikola Tesla", "1. MY EARLY LIFE".

The magazine did not present it as one more technical article. In a box on the same page its editor writes that these questions are "answered in an incomparable manner by Nikola Tesla himself in this, his first article", and continues: "In his autobiography, treating mainly on his early youth, we obtain a good insight into the wonderful life this man has led. It reads like a fairy tale, which has the advantage of being true." (EE, Feb 1919, p. 696.) The word autobiography is the magazine's, and it is the only time the issue uses it.

The instalment is not laid out consecutively. It runs on pp. 696 and 697, breaks, and resumes on p. 743 under a different running head, "NIKOLA TESLA AND HIS INVENTIONS", marked "(Continued from page 697)" (EE, Feb 1919, p. 743). The break is announced at the other end too: the foot of p. 697 reads "(Continued on page 743)". A reader working from the page images will not find the end of the piece by turning two pages forward.

The second instalment is not in this issue. On p. 724 a boxed column headed "ARTICLES SCHEDULED FOR MARCH 'E. E.'" announces the next part under the title 'My Inventions' and the words "No. 2 of a series by Dr. Nikola Tesla. Written exclusively for the ELECTRICAL EXPERIMENTER." (EE, Feb 1919, p. 724.) Anyone who wants the rest of the autobiography needs the March 1919 number, and the February issue says so.

What 'Famous Scientific Illusions' Argues

The other Tesla article in the issue runs from p. 692 to p. 694 and is continued on pp. 728 and 730, marked "(Continued from page 694)". It sets out three things Tesla calls errors among experts. The magazine's own introduction to it asks them as questions: whether the moon rotates on its own axis, "Is the Franklin pointed lightning rod correct in theory and operation?", and whether wireless signals travel through the ether as Hertzian waves or through the earth (EE, Feb 1919, p. 692).

Tesla's answer on the first is a demonstration with a washer and an arrow, that the apparent axial rotation of the moon is an illusion produced by the observer's point of view. On the second he prints a lightning conductor with no points. On the third the magazine's editor summarises him in one line that is worth quoting because it is the same claim the 1894 book makes at greater length: wireless communication is accomplished "ONLY thru the medium of the earth itself" (EE, Feb 1919, p. 692).

The editor did not present the article as one opinion among others. On the contents page, in a note signed with his own name, H. Gernsback argues the case himself. He reports that radio stations are already doing what Tesla says to do: "They now bury their 'aerial' wires in the ground, and lo! they can receive signals twice as far as before", and of the interference every operator complains about, "for there is no 'static' in the ground" (EE, Feb 1919, p. 682). Then he puts a size on the plan: "At some point on the globe he will erect a station powerful enough to charge the whole earth with electricity", and "To do this we need about 10,000 kilowatts" (EE, Feb 1919, p. 682).

And he dates the idea, in the same note: Tesla "has been preaching it ever since he took out his first patents and described his system in 1893" (EE, Feb 1919, p. 682). That is the lecture this site works through in Tesla's wireless power plan of 1893, and the 1894 volume is the written form of the same system. The magazine is not where the claim starts. It is a 1919 restatement of it, in a magazine whose readers were winding their own coils.

That is the connection between this magazine and the rest of this site. The 1894 volume carries the same argument at greater length, with the apparatus and the measurements; the article on Tesla's wireless power plan of 1893 walks through that version, and The 1894 File is the series built on that book. In 1919 Tesla is giving the same argument again, in a magazine that pays for copy and prints it under his own name, for readers who are not physicists.

The Page Rule: Printed Page = Scan + 678

The page images are numbered from 1 and the magazine's own folios start at 681, so the offset is 678: printed page = scan page + 678. Scan 18 is printed p. 696; scan 46 is printed p. 724.

The rule was checked rather than assumed. Of the 84 page images in this scan, 70 expose a folio in the text layer: 20 on a line of their own, 50 in the running head that the magazine prints across the top of every text page. Every one of those 70 equals its scan number plus 678, with no exceptions, and they run from scan 3 (printed p. 681) to scan 82 (printed p. 760).

On the other 14 the text layer gives nothing usable. The front cover and the inside covers carry no folio, the last two images are unnumbered advertising, and ten interior pages have a folio the scanner mangled rather than missing. Printed p. 697 is one of those. Its running head prints "February, 1919", "ELECTRICAL EXPERIMENTER" and "697", and the text layer renders the number as "69/" because the right edge of the page is cut in this scan. Where the text layer is silent, the print agrees with the rule. The numbered body of this issue is pp. 681 to 760.

That is a different document with a different rule from the 1894 book, where the offset is minus 18. Both rules are on this site, they are not interchangeable, and the page that shows how a rule like that is checked across a whole volume is Tesla 1894 Word Search.

How To Cite This Issue

The form this page uses is (EE, Feb 1919, p. 692), where the number is the folio the magazine prints in its own corner. If you are working from page images, convert with the rule above. The cover, the inside covers and the contents page carry no folio and are cited by scan number, which is what this page does for the cover.

The text layer of this scan is poor, and anyone quoting from it should look at the page. It renders the line under Tesla's byline as "IVritten spicially for the Electrical Experimenter"; the print on p. 692 reads "Written specially for the Electrical Experimenter". It renders the magazine's own name as "EMICTBICAL EXPEHIMENTEE" in the colophon, and "Htatic" for "static". Every quotation on this page was read against the print, and where the scan's extracted text differs from the print the print is what is quoted.

The magazine asked for credit, and it is cheap to give. Its colophon on p. 682 says the contents of the magazine "must not be reproduced without giving full credit to the publication". The copyright line printed at the foot of p. 696 reads "Copyright, 1919, by E. P. Co. All rights reserved"; the maximum term for a work published in the United States in 1919 ran out at the end of 2014, so the text has been free to use since then.
